10 · Making it fast

Making it fast: from 33 to 122 frames per second.

Our first test ran at 33 frames per second. The cameras can do 120, and the GPU could handle over 500. Somewhere between the lens and the GPU, three quarters of the frames were being lost. This is the detective story of finding them, one measurement at a time.

First rule: measure before you optimize

The tempting move would have been to speed up the GPU code. It's the fancy part, and it's what everyone talks about. Instead, we added one line to the detector that prints, once a second, how many frames it got and how long each one took.

That line settled it on day one. The detector needed under 2 ms of every frame. At 61 fps, a frame arrives every 16 ms. So the GPU sat idle about 90% of the time, waiting for frames. Making it faster would have changed nothing. The problem was upstream: the camera, and how PhotonVision reads and decodes frames.

Here's that line today, on our Jetson, running 4 cameras at full speed.

Why it matters

Speeding up a part that isn't the bottleneck is wasted work. Find the slowest step first. Here it wasn't even close.

The detective story

1 · 33 fps, 44 ms

The first test: one Thriftiest Cam, 1280×800, detecting a tag at 33 fps with 44 ms latency. Other teams on Chief Delphi reported the same ~32–36 fps "with an idle GPU". Suspicious.

2 · The exposure trap

The slider's "295" meant 29.5 ms of exposure (the units are 100 µs), which caps a camera at 34 fps. At 8.3 ms: 61 fps, 20 ms. Chapter 04 tells the whole trap.

3 · Ask for gray

AprilTags only need brightness, so we asked cscore for grayscale frames instead of converting color in Java (patch 02). Still 62 fps, but Java's CPU fell from 155% to 111% of a core. Only half the fix, as it turned out.

4 · Stop waiting

PhotonVision's Low Latency Mode made the loop wait for each new frame. Each frame took a bit longer to process than the camera's 8.3 ms gap, so after every frame it waited for the next one and skipped the one already waiting. Turning it off: ~100 fps, same latency. (Lab 3 below shows why.)

5 · Two cameras

Add the second camera: 92 and 104 fps, about 23 ms latency, and PhotonVision used 3.3 of the 6 CPU cores. Profiling showed each camera's thread busy 98% of the time, mostly inside one cscore call.

6 · The hidden color decode

Inside that call, cscore decoded every JPEG to full color, then converted it to gray, even though we'd asked for gray: 8.9 ms a frame. Our detector library now decodes the camera's JPEG straight to gray: 2.6 ms. Both cameras: 122 fps, 13 ms. (Lab 2.)

7 · Threads that sleep

OpenCV's helper threads were spinning: checking for work in a tight loop instead of sleeping. Five of them at ~22% of a core each. One setting later, 2 cameras use 1.3 of 6 cores, down from 3.3. (Lab 3.)

8 · The JPEG hardware

The Jetson has dedicated JPEG decoders (NVJPG), separate from the CPU. Decoding there: same 122 fps, PhotonVision went from 0.85 to 0.52 cores on the same scene, back to back. Getting correct frames out of it was its own adventure (Lab 2).

9 · Nobody's watching

PhotonVision prepared the dashboard video for every frame, even with no browser open. Now only while someone watches, at most 30 fps: 0.49 → 0.43 cores. From 33 fps and most of the CPU, to the cameras' full speed on well under one core.

The CPU numbers come from different days and setups, so compare each step's before and after, not steps with each other. All measured on our bench (README "Performance" and docs/VISION-RESEARCH.md).

Lab 1: the USB 2.0 bandwidth budget

Every camera sends its JPEGs over USB 2.0. USB 2.0 splits time into microframes: 8,000 slices a second, each 125 µs long. A video camera doesn't just send when it has data. When it starts, it reserves a fixed number of bytes in every microframe, whether it uses them or not. That's what keeps video smooth: its share is guaranteed.

The catch: the Jetson has one USB 2.0 bus. The four USB-A ports, the USB-C port and the Wi-Fi card's Bluetooth are all branches of it, and together they hold about 6,700 bytes per microframe (about 430 Mbps), whichever ports the cameras are in. Ask for more and the kernel refuses: "No space left on device".

Out of the box, each Thriftiest Cam reserves 3,060 bytes (~196 Mbps) while sending only 4–7 MB/s. Two fit. Our patched camera driver lets us cap a camera's reservation. Try the presets, then build your own robot.

What happens when a camera is squeezed

A camera can't send more than its allocation: the host only lets it use its slice of each microframe. So what does it do when a frame won't fit in time? We tested it by giving two cameras less than they were sending. Both kept their frame rate. They compressed harder instead: smaller, lower-quality JPEGs, taking longer to cross USB.

For AprilTag cameras we keep them out of the squeeze (under 90% used) with room to spare ("fits" 1.3× or more). The price of going smaller is latency: a 60 KB frame takes 5.9 ms to cross at 1,280 bytes, and 8.0 ms at 944. It doesn't hurt timestamp accuracy, because the driver stamps a frame when its first packet arrives.

The USB-C port doesn't add bandwidth

It feels like a separate port should have its own budget. It doesn't: it's another branch of the same USB 2.0 bus. Real ways to get more: a USB 3 camera (it streams on the separate USB 3 bus), or a PCIe USB controller card in the dev kit's empty M.2 Key M slot, which would add a second USB 2.0 bus (untested).

Lab 2: the decoding race

A JPEG is a compressed picture: about 50 KB instead of the 1 MB of raw pixels. Before the detector can look at it, it has to be decoded back into pixels. That takes several steps, and it's where most of our CPU was going.

The frequency trick

JPEG cuts the image into 8×8 blocks and stores each block not as 64 pixels, but as 64 frequencies: how much of each checkerboard-like pattern to add up. Most of the picture lives in the first few; the fine detail in the rest is rounded off, which is where the space is saved. Decoding runs this backwards with the IDCT. Slide to rebuild a block of a tag's corner from its frequencies.

Why color costs more: a color JPEG stores brightness (Y) plus two color channels (Cb, Cr) at lower resolution. cscore decoded our mono camera's frames into a full blue-green-red image (three identical channels, for a gray camera!), three bytes per pixel, then averaged it back to gray. Decoding straight to gray skips building and undoing that color image, and skips the color channels' IDCT and upsampling when a JPEG has them.

The hardware decoder that froze

The obvious way to call NVIDIA's JPEG library (the way another FRC codebase does it) looked amazing in a speed test. It also returned the first frame, over and over, while reporting success every time. We only caught it by decoding every frame both ways and comparing them pixel by pixel.

We call it differently now. And because a mistake here would be invisible, a safety net stays on forever: every ~2 s, one frame per camera is decoded again on the CPU and compared. Any difference switches the hardware off. On Sept 25 the log read checks since start 19925 ok, 0 differ.

250 KB a frame

Then PhotonVision grew to 5.6 GB of memory within minutes, and Linux killed it. Twice. NVIDIA's library leaks about 250 KB on every frame unless it's put in its "MJPEG" mode, which NVIDIA's own example code does without explaining why. One setting fixed it: memory stayed flat for 8 minutes and about 116,000 frames.

Our tests had only ever run for a minute at a time, so they never saw it. Now the test fails if memory grows, and the health check warns if PhotonVision passes 2.5 GB or was killed for running out.

Lesson

Run new code for a long time and watch its memory, not just its speed.

Lab 3: threads, spinning and sleeping

A thread is one sequence of instructions a program is working through. PhotonVision has dozens: one per camera for the pipeline, one for each stream, helpers for math, networking, logging. The Jetson has 6 CPU cores, so at most 6 threads run at any instant.

The operating system's scheduler shares the cores out, switching threads many times a second, so everything seems to run at once. A thread with nothing to do should sleep: tell the scheduler "wake me when there's work", and give its core away.

Some threads instead spin (busy-wait): they check "is there work yet?" in a tight loop. That reacts a hair faster, but it burns a core doing nothing. OpenCV's helper pool spins for a while after each job by default. For us that was five threads at ~22% of a core each.

Analogy: waiting for a pizza

Spinning is standing at the door, checking the peephole every second. Sleeping is sitting on the couch until the doorbell rings. The pizza arrives at the same time either way, but only one of them lets you do your homework meanwhile.

Low Latency Mode: waiting for the next frame

A related trap. With Low Latency Mode on, PhotonVision's loop waited for a new frame after finishing each one. If the work takes even a little longer than the gap between frames, the frame that arrived during the work is thrown away, and the loop waits for the one after. Drag the processing time and watch the frame rate fall off a cliff.

The smaller lessons

⏱️

Short exposure, lower cutoff

Short exposure means less motion blur but a darker image and lower decision margins (about 44 at 3 ms, 118 at 8.3 ms). We run 5 ms with the cutoff at 15. Chapter 04.

📷

0.6 of a core per camera

After the decode fix, each camera at 122 fps costs about 0.6 of a CPU core (it was 1.4), and less with the JPEG hardware. That's what makes 4 cameras fit on 6 cores.

🖥️

The dashboard costs CPU

Each open camera stream makes the Jetson shrink, draw and encode video. Four open streams added about half a core and 1–2 ms to the slowest frames. Close it before measuring.

🔁

Measure twice

The same settings varied by up to 2 ms from one restart to the next. One run made a setting look like a big win; eleven showed maybe 0.5 ms. Chapter 08.

Chapter 10 recap
  • Measure first: a one-line stats log showed the GPU was idle, so the problem was the camera and the CPU path.
  • 33 → 122 fps came from exposure (29.5 ms capped us at 34 fps), not waiting for frames, decoding straight to gray, sleeping threads, JPEG hardware and a lazier dashboard stream.
  • All USB 2.0 ports share about 6,700 bytes per 125 µs microframe. Capped cameras fit 4 plus a 5th; a squeezed camera compresses harder and adds latency.
  • Decoding color you don't need is expensive. Hardware decoders are fast but must be checked pixel by pixel, and long tests catch leaks.
  • Spinning threads burn cores doing nothing; a blocking wait can halve your frame rate.
