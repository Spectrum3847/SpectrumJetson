12 · The math of seeing

From corners to pose.

The detector hands over four corner pixels per tag. From just those, plus the calibration from the last chapter, the robot works out where it's standing on the field. Here's how, why it sometimes gets it badly wrong, and why more tags fix it.

If you know how big it is, you know how far

Hold your thumb out at arm's length and it covers a car across the street. Things look smaller the farther away they are, in exact proportion: twice as far, half as big. That's similar triangles, and it's the heart of the pinhole model from chapter 11.

Every AprilTag on an FRC field is printed the same size: the black square is 165.1 mm (6.5 in) across. So if the camera measures how many pixels tall a tag looks, it can solve for the distance:

distance = fx × real size / size in pixels

There's a catch. Far away, a tag is only a couple of dozen pixels tall, and a one-pixel mistake in the corners is a bigger fraction of that. Slide the tag back and watch the error bar grow: it grows with the square of the distance.

Three clues in four corners

Size gives distance. Two more clues come free with it:

  • Where the tag is in the picture gives its direction. A tag right of center is to the camera's right, by an angle you can compute from the calibration: angle = atan((u − cx) / fx).
  • How squashed it looks gives its angle. A square seen from the side becomes a narrow trapezoid: the near edge looks taller than the far edge.

PnP: all the clues at once

Doing the clues one at a time is shaky. The real method uses all four corners together. It's called PnP, Perspective-n-Point, and its question is:

Where must the camera be, and which way must it face, so that the tag's four real corners land exactly on these four pixels?

It knows three things: the corners' real 3D positions on the tag (a 165.1 mm square), where they appeared in the image, and the camera's intrinsics. The answer is the camera's pose relative to the tag: 3 numbers for position and 3 for rotation.

Every FRC AprilTag is the same size, so its four corners say a lot: its size gives distance, its place in the picture gives direction, and how squashed it looks gives its angle. PnP (Perspective-n-Point) uses all four corners at once to find where the camera must be. The field layout and the camera's mount on the robot then give the robot's pose on the field.

The catch: a small, far tag seen nearly straight on can fit two poses, turned left or right, on opposite sides of the tag (pose ambiguity). With two or more tags in view, PhotonVision's multi-tag mode solves all the corners at once, and only one answer fits. Drag the robot far away, nearly straight on, and watch for flips. From the tag to the field to the robot

PnP gives the camera's pose relative to the tag. Two more facts, chained like links, give the robot's pose on the field:

The chain in code

In WPILib terms, with PhotonLib's names:

// 1. where the tag is on the field (from the layout JSON) Pose3d tagPose = layout.getTagPose(target.getFiducialId()).get(); // 2. PnP's answer: the tag as seen from the camera Transform3d camToTag = target.getBestCameraToTarget(); // 3. walk the chain backwards: tag → camera → robot Pose3d camPose = tagPose.transformBy(camToTag.inverse()); Pose3d robotPose = camPose.transformBy(robotToCamera.inverse());

PhotonLib's PhotonUtils.estimateFieldToRobotAprilTag does the same thing. The extrinsics from chapter 11 are the last link, so a camera mount that's off by 1° shows up directly in the robot's pose.

Tag ambiguity: two answers that both fit

Here's the catch. A small or distant tag seen nearly straight on has almost no perspective: its near edge is only a hair taller than its far edge. Turn it 20° left or 20° right and the two pictures are nearly identical.

The corners are only known to about a pixel, so PnP often finds two poses that both explain the corners: one with the tag turned one way, one with it turned the other. For a flat square this is a known property of the math, called the planar pose ambiguity. The two answers put the camera at the same distance but on opposite sides of the tag's center line, which can put the robot meters from where it really is.

PhotonVision measures how sure it is with a number called pose ambiguity: the reprojection error of the best solution divided by the error of the alternate one.

  • Near 0: the alternate fits much worse, so the best one is trustworthy.
  • Near 1: both fit equally well; it's a coin flip.
  • PhotonVision's docs suggest rejecting single-tag poses with ambiguity above 0.2.

A flip is worse than noise

Noise nudges the pose a few centimeters. A flip teleports it. If robot code feeds a flipped pose to its pose estimator, the robot suddenly believes it's somewhere else on the field, and auto paths or aiming follow that belief. That's why single-tag poses need an ambiguity check, and why the next idea matters so much.

Multi-tag: more corners, one answer

With two or more tags in view, there are 8 or more corners spread across the image, and the field layout says exactly where every one of them sits relative to the others. Now the two-answer problem goes away: turning the camera to fit one tag's flip would throw the other tag's corners far off.

PhotonVision's multi-tag mode solves one PnP for all the corners at once, directly in field coordinates, on the Jetson. It needs 3D mode, a calibration, and the same field layout as the robot code.

What we did: multi-tag on by default, bad tags left out

On by default (photonvision-06): a new camera starts with multi-tag on, and 3D turns itself on as soon as a calibration is saved for its resolution. We added this after finding that both bench cameras had multi-tag off. Leaving bad tags out (photonvision-22): if a tag is mounted wrong at an event, type its number on the Settings page (or robot code publishes it on /photonvision/excludedTags) and every camera's multi-tag solve ignores it. The tag is still reported with its own single-tag pose. Try the "mounted 15 cm off" box above to see why this matters: one bad tag drags every multi-tag answer.

On the robot

The Jetson sends its results to the robot, where PhotonLib's PhotonPoseEstimator turns them into robot poses: the Jetson's multi-tag answer when there is one, otherwise the least ambiguous single tag. Its other methods add the gyro; chapter 13 covers them and how they match Limelight's MegaTag names.

Trust depends on distance

A vision pose isn't simply right or wrong. It's a best guess with some spread, and the spread grows fast with distance (you saw why: fewer pixels per tag) and shrinks with more tags. Robot code tells the pose estimator how much to trust each measurement with standard deviations: a small number means "trust this a lot".

The robot's drivetrain pose estimator blends vision with odometry using the idea behind a Kalman filter. Each source is a guess with a spread. The blend leans toward whichever is more certain, and the combined guess is more certain than either alone.

A common rule of thumb, from team 6328 and listed in our research notes: standard deviation ≈ 0.01 × distance² / tags² meters. PhotonVision itself doesn't publish standard deviations, so this lives in robot code.

Feeding the pose estimator

A typical loop, per camera, per new result:

for (var result : camera.getAllUnreadResults()) { var est = estimator.estimateCoprocMultiTagPose(result); if (est.isEmpty()) est = estimator.estimateLowestAmbiguityPose(result); est.ifPresent(e -> { // reject flips: single-tag ambiguity, off-field poses... double xy = 0.01 * d * d / (n * n); // d = average tag distance, n = tags drivetrain.addVisionMeasurement(e.estimatedPose.toPose2d(), e.timestampSeconds, VecBuilder.fill(xy, xy, theta)); }); }

Three details matter. Every frame: getAllUnreadResults() returns about 2 frames per camera per 20 ms loop at 90 fps, so none are dropped. The timestamp is when the frame was captured, not when it arrived, so the estimator can apply it to the right moment in the past (chapter 14). Gates: our off-season robot code already rejects bad poses and uses std-dev tiers; those move over, retuned, because PhotonVision computes ambiguity differently from Limelight.
