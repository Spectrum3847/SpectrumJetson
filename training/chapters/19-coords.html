Coordinate systems.

Most "vision bugs" in robot code aren't vision bugs. They're a minus sign, a transform pointing the wrong way, or degrees where radians belong. Here are the rules in one place, so the numbers going into robot code mean what you think they mean.

Everything uses WPILib's rules. The field: the origin is always the blue corner, on both alliances. x points toward the red wall, y to the left (as the blue drivers see it), z up. Metres and radians. Angles: counter-clockwise is positive. The robot: x forward, y left, z up, origin at the robot's centre on the floor.

The one number robot code must get right is robotToCamera: where each camera sits on the robot. A camera tilted up has negative pitch, the angles are in radians, and it points from the robot to the camera. Try it:

The field: one origin for everyone

A pose is just three numbers: x, y and heading. They only mean something if everyone agrees where zero is and which way is positive. WPILib, PhotonVision, PathPlanner, Choreo and AdvantageScope all use the same rule, so pick it up once:

  • The origin is the blue corner. It's the right-hand corner of the blue alliance wall, as the blue drivers see it, on the floor. Always, even when we're on the red alliance.
  • x points away from the blue wall, toward the red one. y points left as the blue drivers see it. z points up.
  • Metres and radians, everywhere in code. Degrees and inches are only for people.

That's called NWU: x "north", y "west", z up. It's a right-handed system: point your right hand's fingers along x, curl them toward y, and your thumb points along z.

On the 2026 AndyMark field, the one we'll play on in October, the far corner is at x = 16.518 m, y = 8.043 m. The centre of the field is (8.259, 4.022). The blue hub is at about x = 4.6 m and the red hub at about 11.9 m.

Angles: counter-clockwise is positive

Every angle in WPILib follows the right-hand rule. Point your right thumb along an axis, and your fingers curl the way a positive turn goes.

  • Yaw is a turn around z (thumb up). Seen from above, that's counter-clockwise: turning left makes yaw bigger. Heading 0 faces the red wall, +90° faces the left side wall.
  • Pitch is a turn around y (thumb to the robot's left). Your fingers curl so the front tips down. So a camera tilted up is negative pitch. This one catches everybody.
  • Roll is a turn around x (thumb forward): the left side rises.

In code the angles are radians: 15° is 0.262 rad, and 1 rad is 57.3°. Let WPILib convert for you with Units.degreesToRadians(15) or Rotation2d.fromDegrees(15), and never type a bare degree number into a constructor.

Rotation3d: roll, pitch, yaw, in that order

new Rotation3d(roll, pitch, yaw) takes radians and applies the turns around the fixed axes in the order x, then y, then z (WPILib calls these extrinsic rotations). That's the same as turning around the object's own axes in the opposite order: yaw first, then pitch around the camera's new left axis, then roll around its new forward axis. For a camera, that matches how you'd describe it: "turned 30° left, then tilted 15° up".

Order matters once two angles are non-zero: yaw 90° then pitch 20° is not the same as pitch 20° then yaw 90°. As long as you build the rotation from the constructor and read it back with getX() (roll), getY() (pitch) and getZ() (yaw), the order is handled for you.

Rotation2d is only the yaw. Pose3d.toPose2d() keeps x, y and yaw and drops z, roll and pitch, which is what the drivetrain's pose estimator wants.

Four frames you'll meet

A frame is an origin plus three axes. Vision code hops between four of them, and every one uses WPILib's x-forward, y-left, z-up pattern:

Field

Origin: the blue corner, on the floor.

  • x toward the red wall
  • y left, as blue sees it
  • z up

Robot

Origin: the robot's centre, on the floor.

  • x forward
  • y left
  • z up

Camera

Origin: the lens.

  • x out of the lens
  • y the camera's left
  • z the camera's up

AprilTag

Origin: the tag's centre.

  • x straight out of the printed face
  • y to your right as you face the tag
  • z up

A robot facing a tag head-on sees it straight ahead (+x) and turned 180° (its x points back at the robot). PhotonVision draws all of these with the same colours as this page: x red, y green, z blue, in the 3D view and the target overlay.

OpenCV's camera frame, and why you never see it

Inside PhotonVision, OpenCV's solvePnP works in its own camera frame: x right, y down, z out of the lens, the way image pixels are laid out (u grows right, v grows down). The tag's frame in OpenCV is different again.

Before the result leaves the Jetson, PhotonVision rotates it into the WPILib-style frames above, so getBestCameraToTarget() is already x-forward, y-left, z-up. You only need OpenCV's frame if you read raw corner pixels and do your own math: then remember pixel v grows downward, which is why a target above the centre of the image has positive pitch even though its v is smaller. Tick "OpenCV axes" in the lab below to see both on the same camera.

robotToCamera: where the camera sits

Each camera needs one Transform3d in robot code: the move and turn that take you from the robot's origin to the camera's lens. PhotonLib's PhotonPoseEstimator takes it in its constructor (chapter 18). Every number is in the robot frame:

  • x: how far the lens is in front of the robot's centre (negative = behind).
  • y: how far it is to the left of centre (negative = right).
  • z: how high the lens is above the floor, not above the frame rails.
  • yaw: which way it faces. 0 = forward, +90° = out the left side, 180° = backward.
  • pitch: tilt. Up is negative.
  • roll: usually 0, unless the camera is mounted sideways or twisted.

The name tells you the direction: robot to camera. PhotonLib finds the camera's pose on the field from the tags, then walks back to the robot with robotToCamera.inverse() (chapter 12 shows the whole chain). Get any number wrong and every pose from that camera is wrong by the same amount.

Measuring it on the real robot

Start from CAD. Put a point at the robot's centre on the floor plane and read off the lens position and the mount angles. CAD is usually right to a few millimetres, if the robot was built like the CAD.

Check with a tape measure. Measure from the robot's centre (halfway between the frame rails, both ways) to the middle of the lens, and from the floor up. Use a digital angle gauge or a phone level on the camera's face for pitch. Write the numbers in metres and degrees with the sign: "0.26 forward, 0.26 left, 0.45 up, yawed 30° left, tilted 15° up" becomes (0.26, 0.26, 0.45), yaw +30°, pitch −15°.

Name it after the port. Our cameras are named after their USB ports (TopLeft, TopRight, BottomLeft, BottomRight), so give each camera's transform the same name in robot code. Swapping two cables swaps two mounts, and every pose from both cameras goes wrong. Chapter 20 covers setting up the names.

What we built: two ways to check it

Camera mount estimate (photonvision-17). With the robot level on the floor and 2+ tags in view, the Targets tab shows each camera's height, pitch and roll, averaged over 100 samples. They use robotToCamera's signs (tilted up = negative pitch), so compare them straight with robot code's z, pitch and roll. Robot code can read them at /photonvision/<camera>/mount and warn when a camera gets bumped.

Field calibration (photonvision-30). Push the robot to 10–20 spots and the Jetson solves every camera's full mount, x, y and yaw included, and gives a Java snippet. For that, robot code publishes each camera's robotToCamera at /photonvision/<camera>/robotToCamera. See chapter 11.

Target yaw and pitch: the other sign convention

PhotonLib gives two kinds of answers for each target. The 2D ones, getYaw() and getPitch(), are angles in degrees from the centre of the image to the target. The 3D one, getBestCameraToTarget(), is a Transform3d in the camera frame, in metres and radians.

Here's the trap: getYaw() is positive to the right (clockwise), the opposite of every other angle in WPILib. getPitch() is positive up. We checked PhotonVision's source at the version on our robot: the yaw is atan((u − cx) / fx), and pixel u grows to the right. PhotonLib's own PhotonUtils notes that "Photon returns CW-positive". Some of PhotonVision's docs and one Javadoc comment say "positive left". They're wrong.

Yes, the last two pitches have opposite signs. calculateDistanceToTargetMeters takes the camera's tilt "positive up", so pass -robotToCamera.getRotation().getY().

The two agree if you convert: for a tag, getYaw() is minus the angle of the 3D translation, −atan2(y, x), and getPitch() is atan2(z, √(x² + y²)). Both are measured from the camera's axis, not the robot's, so a camera yawed 30° needs that added before you aim the robot.

That's why the classic "turn toward the tag" code has a minus sign in it. A tag to the right gives a positive yaw, but turning right is a negative (clockwise) rotation speed.

// Aim at a tag: yaw is + to the RIGHT, // rotation speed is + to the LEFT (CCW). double turn = -kP * target.getYaw(); // The same angle from the 3D transform (radians, + left): Translation3d t = target.getBestCameraToTarget().getTranslation(); double leftRad = Math.atan2(t.getY(), t.getX()); // Units.radiansToDegrees(leftRad) == -target.getYaw()

Red alliance: flip the plans, not the poses

Because the origin never moves, vision poses need no flipping. PhotonVision on the Jetson always measures from the blue corner, and so does the pose estimator. A robot 2 m in front of the red wall is at x = 14.5 m, whichever alliance it's on.

What does need flipping is anything we wrote down for blue: auto paths, scoring spots, aim targets like "the centre of our hub". The 2026 field is the same when turned 180° around its centre, so red's version of a blue pose is (16.518 − x, 8.043 − y, heading + 180°). PathPlanner and Choreo can do this for paths. Also, for red drivers, "push the stick forward" means −x on the field, so field-centric driving flips the joystick on red.

Don't move the origin

Old robot code sometimes called layout.setOrigin(OriginPosition.kRedAllianceWallRightSide) on red. Don't. The Jetson's poses are always blue-origin, so the robot code's layout must be too. And load the right field: in WPILib 2027 alpha-6, AprilTagFields.kDefaultField is the welded 2026 field (16.541 × 8.069 m). Ours is AndyMark: AprilTagFieldLayout.loadField(AprilTagFields.k2026RebuiltAndymark), the same layout loaded in PhotonVision.

The mount in code, the 2027 way

In WPILib 2027 the packages moved from edu.wpi.first.math to org.wpilib.math. PhotonLib 2027.0.0-alpha-2 uses the new ones:

import org.wpilib.math.geometry.*; import org.wpilib.math.util.Units; import org.wpilib.vision.apriltag.AprilTagFieldLayout; import org.wpilib.vision.apriltag.AprilTagFields; // TopLeft: 0.26 m forward, 0.26 m left, 0.45 m up, turned 30° left, tilted 15° UP static final Transform3d TOP_LEFT = new Transform3d( new Translation3d(0.26, 0.26, 0.45), new Rotation3d(0, Units.degreesToRadians(-15), Units.degreesToRadians(30))); static final AprilTagFieldLayout LAYOUT = AprilTagFieldLayout.loadField(AprilTagFields.k2026RebuiltAndymark); // Need camera → robot instead? Invert it, don't retype it. Transform3d cameraToRobot = TOP_LEFT.inverse();
