07 · The computer

A frame's journey.

In about 15 milliseconds, one camera frame travels down a cable, into memory, through a JPEG engine, across 1,024 GPU cores and out to the robot. Let's ride along, then see why a GPU beats a Raspberry Pi's CPU at this job.

On the Jetson, the CPU, GPU and JPEG engines share one memory (unified memory), so a picture is never copied to a separate graphics card. The slow part is the USB cable, so the camera sends each frame as a small JPEG.

First, memory

A workbench and a filing cabinet

RAM is your workbench: everything you're working on right now, within arm's reach, but cleared when the power goes off. The SSD is the filing cabinet across the room: huge and permanent, but you have to walk to it. Caches are the few tools in your hand: tiny, but instant.

Every step of a frame's journey reads from memory and writes back to it. The Jetson's RAM moves up to 102 GB per second. A whole 1280×800 gray image is about 1 MB (one byte per pixel), so at that speed it could cross in about 10 microseconds.

The slow road is the USB cable. Each of our cameras gets a 10.2 MB/s share of the USB 2.0 bus. A raw 1 MB frame would take 100 ms to squeeze through, far too slow for 120 frames a second. That's why the camera compresses every frame to a JPEG of about 50 KB first (our real frames: 49.7–60.4 KB): then it crosses in about 5 ms.

1 · The shutter

Light in, for 5 ms

All pixels expose at once for 5 ms.

The camera's global shutter opens every pixel at once for 5 ms (our exposure setting), then closes. The frame's timestamp marks the middle of that window, when the tag was really where the picture shows it.

2 · Down the cable

JPEG in 125 µs slices ≈ 8.1 ms

The camera streams the JPEG over USB, over about 8 ms.

The camera compresses the frame to a JPEG of about 50 KB (one of our real frames is 52,801 bytes) and sends it in USB microframes: up to 1280 bytes every 125 µs, the booking our driver gives it (chapter 06). It doesn't send it all at once: it streams the JPEG out while its sensor is still reading out, over about one frame period. We timed it with the driver's own log: 8.1 ms from the first packet to the last, on every camera. At our cap a 34 KB frame could cross in 3.4 ms, so the camera sets the pace, not USB.

One delay we still haven't measured: any time between the end of the exposure and the camera's first packet. It's on our list for the robot.

3 · Straight into RAM

No CPU needed

The USB controller writes it into RAM by itself.

The USB controller writes each packet directly into a buffer in RAM. This is DMA: hardware copying data by itself while the CPU does other things. The CPU never touches the bytes on the way in.

4 · The driver

"Buffer ready"

The uvcvideo driver marks it ready.

When the last packet lands, the kernel's uvcvideo driver marks the buffer done. The frame's time comes from the camera's own clock, which the driver converts to the Jetson's (chapter 14); PhotonVision later moves that stamp back to mid-exposure. Our driver also looks for finished packets every 2 ms instead of every 4, which gets each frame to PhotonVision 1.2 ms sooner.

5 · PhotonVision

Grab the raw JPEG

PhotonVision picks up the raw JPEG.

The camera's thread in PhotonVision picks up the raw JPEG through cscore, untouched (why that matters: chapter 10).

6 · Unpack

NVJPG decodes to gray ≈ 2.8 ms

A hardware engine unpacks it to a 1 MB gray image.

The JPEG goes to one of the two NVJPG hardware engines. It unpacks the 50 KB into a 1 MB gray image, one byte per pixel, while the CPU waits or does other work. Measured: 2.8 ms with 5 cameras sharing the two engines, and PhotonVision fell from 0.85 to 0.52 CPU cores with two cameras.

7 · Shared memory

To the GPU, without a trip

The GPU reads it from the same memory: no copy.

On a desktop PC the graphics card has its own memory, so every image must be copied across the PCIe slot and back. On the Jetson the CPU and GPU share the same RAM: unified memory. Copies are cheap: the detector's upload took just 0.16 ms. Our decoder now skips even that: it also leaves each gray frame in a GPU buffer, where the detector picks it up (1.55 → 1.32 ms a frame with 4 cameras).

8 · The GPU

Find the tags ≈ 1.2–2 ms

1,024 cores find the tags' corners.

CUDA programs (kernel (GPU)s) run on all 1,024 GPU cores: find edges, group them into outlines, fit four-sided shapes. Then CPU threads read each candidate's bits. Out come a few numbers: each tag's ID and its four corner positions. Chapter 08 opens this box.

9 · Back to the CPU

Corners → pose

The CPU turns corners into a pose.

The CPU takes the corners and works out where the camera must be to see the tags that way (OpenCV's solvePnP, chapter 12). Small math, a perfect job for one fast CPU core.

10 · To the robot

A NetworkTables packet

Off to the robot over Ethernet: about 14.5 ms in all.

The result (tag IDs, corners, pose, timestamp) goes out over Ethernet to the SystemCore through NetworkTables. PhotonVision's dashboard shows 14.3–15.2 ms per camera, from mid-exposure to the result.

11 · And again

120 times a second, per camera

A new frame starts every 8.3 ms.

While this frame was on its trip, the next one was already being exposed. At 120 fps a new frame starts every 8.3 ms, so two or three are in flight at once, each at a different station.

One thread per camera

That was one frame from one camera. With several cameras, PhotonVision gives each one its own thread, called a VisionRunner. Each one loops forever: grab a frame, decode, detect, solve, publish. The Linux scheduler spreads the threads over the 6 CPU cores, and they all share the one GPU.

That's why a second camera costs about as much as the first, and why a slow camera can't hold up the others. Chapter 08 digs into what happens when four cameras line up for the GPU at the same moment, and chapter 10 into how we got five running at full speed.

Why a GPU beats a Raspberry Pi's CPU

A CPU core is a genius: it can do anything, fast, one step at a time. A GPU core is a helper that only follows simple instructions, and more slowly. But the Jetson has 1,024 of them, and they all work at once.

Lots of image work looks like "do the same small thing to every pixel": is it brighter than its neighbors? Is it part of an edge? A 1280×800 frame has 1,024,000 pixels. Four CPU cores must take turns through them. 1,024 GPU cores can each take a small patch.

The honest fine print

  • Not every step splits up. Tracing an outline, sorting, and decisions that depend on the previous answer run one step at a time. Chapter 08's race shows it: on a chain of steps, one CPU core beats the whole GPU crowd. Real detectors mix both kinds of work.
  • CPU cores cheat a little, too. Each Arm core can do the same operation on 16 bytes at once (SIMD). CPU AprilTag code uses that, and it's fast.
  • Moving data costs time. Handing work to the GPU and getting answers back isn't free. Unified memory makes it cheap on the Jetson (0.16 ms), but a tiny job isn't worth sending.
  • The GPU is mostly idle for us. With 2 cameras at 122 fps, it's about 12% busy (peaks under 25%). With 4, about a quarter (25.7%); with 5 facing bright ceiling lights, 44%. The detector takes ~1.2–2 ms a frame, fast enough for 500+ fps. Our real limits were elsewhere (chapter 10).
  • The JPEG engines work harder than the GPU. In a 4-camera test (Sept 26), the GPU averaged 17% busy while each of the two NVJPG engines averaged about 37%.

Comparing fairly with an Orange Pi 5

Many FRC teams run stock PhotonVision on an Orange Pi 5, which finds AprilTags on its CPU cores. It works, and it's cheaper. The CPU simply has more to do per frame, so frame rate, resolution and the number of cameras have to be balanced against each other. On the Jetson, the heavy per-pixel work moves to the GPU and JPEG engines, which is why our CPU has room for five cameras at 122 fps at full resolution. We haven't benchmarked an Orange Pi ourselves, so we don't quote numbers for it.

Chapter 7 recap
  • A frame goes: exposure → JPEG over USB (~8.1 ms, paced by the camera) → DMA into RAM → uvcvideo → PhotonVision → NVJPG decode (~2.8 ms) → GPU detect (~1.2–2 ms) → CPU pose solve → NetworkTables. About 14.5 ms from mid-exposure.
  • The CPU, GPU and JPEG engines share one 8 GB RAM at 102 GB/s (unified memory), so there's no slow copy to a graphics card.
  • GPUs win on work that splits into many identical pieces, like per-pixel steps. Sequential work still belongs on the CPU.
  • Each camera has its own thread; they share the GPU, which is only about a quarter busy with four cameras.
