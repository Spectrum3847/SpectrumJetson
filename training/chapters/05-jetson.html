05 · The computer

A supercomputer the size of a sandwich.

The Jetson Orin Nano Super fits in your hand and runs on about 10 watts, yet it can do 67 trillion AI math operations a second. Let's take one apart and see what's inside.

What is a single-board computer?

Your laptop has a CPU, memory (RAM), storage, a graphics chip and a pile of ports. A SBC squeezes all of that onto one small board, with no screen, keyboard or case. You talk to it over a network cable or a USB cable.

NVIDIA's Jetson line is built for robots and cameras. Its special skill is a big GPU: the same kind of chip that draws video games, which is also brilliant at looking at pictures. That's exactly what finding AprilTags needs.

Ours is the Jetson Orin Nano Super Developer Kit: NVIDIA's small computer module plugged into a carrier board full of ports, with a heatsink and fan on top. The kit costs $249.

Step 1 · The whole kit

About 10 × 8 cm

Fan on top, ports along the front.

From the top you see the black fan and heatsink. Along the front edge are the ports: power, DisplayPort, four USB, Ethernet and USB-C. Everything sits on a plastic stand.

It's a developer kit: a board for trying things out. Many robots use it as is, and so do we.

Step 2 · Cooling

Heatsink and fan

They carry the chip's heat away. Our fan runs on NVIDIA's quiet profile.

Every computer turns its electricity into heat. The metal heatsink soaks up the chip's heat and spreads it into thin fins, and the fan blows air through them. A leaf spring presses the heatsink onto the chip.

The chip slows itself down at 99 °C to protect itself (throttling), and shuts down at 104 °C. Our fan runs on NVIDIA's quiet profile: NVIDIA's fan program, nvfancontrol, picks its speed from the chip's temperature. On the bench it turned at just 775 rpm, and the chip sat at 43 °C. Full speed (about 5,600 rpm) is one setting away: FAN=full.

Step 3 · The brain

The Orin Nano module

The actual computer: the SoC chip and 8 GB of memory.

This small board, 69.6 × 45 mm, is the actual computer. It holds the SoC (one chip with the CPU, GPU and more) and 8 GB of memory. Everything else in the kit just feeds it power and connects it to the world.

NVIDIA's CAD shows it as a simple block, so you don't see the chips. We'll look inside the SoC after the tour.

Step 4 · The socket

Plugs in like laptop RAM

It plugs in like a laptop's memory stick.

The module's gold edge has 260 contacts, and it slides into a 260-pin SO-DIMM socket, the same shape laptops use for memory sticks. Two screws hold it down.

That makes the module swappable: the same carrier could take a faster Orin NX module. Power, PCIe lanes, USB, Ethernet and camera signals all pass through those contacts.

Step 5 · The carrier board

Power and ports (P3768)

It feeds the module power and wires it to real ports.

The big board underneath is the carrier board. It turns the power from the barrel jack into the voltages the module needs, and wires the module's signals out to real connectors. The tiny parts scattered across it are power regulators, the USB hub chip, LEDs and support chips.

Step 6 · Ports

What plugs in where

USB-A for cameras, Ethernet to the robot, USB-C, DisplayPort, power.

  • 4× USB 3.2 Type-A (10 Gbps): our cameras. All four hang off one hub.
  • Gigabit Ethernet: the cable to the robot, static IP 10.85.15.15.
  • USB-C (data only): flashing and backups from a laptop.
  • DisplayPort: the only video output. On the robot, nothing's plugged in.
  • DC jack (5.5 × 2.5 mm): power in.
Step 7 · For tinkering

Camera connectors and GPIO pins

Ribbon-camera connectors and a 40-pin header for sensors.

On the left edge are two MIPI CSI-2 connectors for ribbon-cable cameras. We use USB cameras instead, so they're empty.

On the right is the 40-pin header: general-purpose pins, laid out like a Raspberry Pi's, for LEDs, sensors and buttons. A smaller 12-pin button header hides under the module; jumping two of its pins puts the Jetson in recovery mode for flashing (chapter 06).

Step 8 · Underneath

The SSD: where Linux lives

Underneath, a 256 GB SSD holds Linux.

Flip the board over. The small card is an NVMe SSD, plugged into an M.2 Key M slot. Ours holds 256 GB, and the Jetson boots Linux from it. We don't use an SD card at all: the SSD is much faster.

Not every SSD is tough, though. Our first one, a budget drive, died after 21 power cuts. The story, and how the SSD is split up now, is in chapter 16.

There are two Key M slots: a long one (2280) with 4 PCIe lanes, where our SSD goes, and a short one (2230) with 2 lanes, which is empty. (The CAD draws a short 2230 drive.)

Step 9 · Underneath

Wi-Fi and Bluetooth

Wi-Fi and Bluetooth, off at events.

The second card is Wi-Fi and Bluetooth, in the M.2 Key E slot, with two flat antennas. We use Wi-Fi in the shop and switch it off for events.

A surprise we measured: the card's Bluetooth talks over USB 2.0, on the same USB 2.0 bus as all our cameras (chapter 10 explains why that matters).

Inside the SoC: one chip, many workers

A desktop PC spreads its brains over several chips: a CPU, a separate graphics card with its own memory, and more. The Jetson puts almost all of it on one chip, a System on a Chip. That saves space and power, and it lets every part share the same memory.

Think of a workshop

The CPU is 6 skilled machinists who can do any job, one step at a time. The GPU is 1,024 helpers who can only do simple jobs, but all at once. NVJPG is a machine that only unpacks JPEGs. They all share one big workbench: the RAM.

CPU

6 Arm cores, 1.7 GHz

Cortex-A78AE cores run Linux, Java and PhotonVision. In MAXN SUPER mode we lock all six at 1728 MHz, so timing never wobbles.

1.5 MB L2 + 4 MB L3 cache
GPU

1,024 CUDA cores

NVIDIA Ampere, at 1020 MHz. It runs our AprilTag detector in about 2 ms a frame. The 32 Tensor Cores speed up neural networks (game pieces).

8 groups of 128 cores

8 GB LPDDR5

A 128-bit-wide path moving up to 102 GB a second. The CPU and GPU share it: unified memory, the star of chapter 07.

Two NVJPG JPEG decoders (we use them, chapter 10) and an NVDEC video decoder. There's no hardware video encoder on the Orin Nano.

Simplified: real chips have more blocks and links. The USB wiring (one controller, one USB 2.0 bus with three branches) is what we measured on our Jetson.

What we found: there is only one USB 2.0 bus

The four USB-A ports, the USB-C port and the Wi-Fi card's Bluetooth all share one USB 2.0 bus, so every camera shares one bandwidth budget. That decides how many cameras fit: chapter 10 tells that story.

You can see the one-bus rule in Linux's own list of USB devices:

  1. Bus 02 is the USB 3 bus (10000M = 10 Gbps). Only the hub's USB 3 half is on it, and none of our cameras.
  2. Bus 01 is the USB 2.0 bus (480M = 480 Mbps). Everything under it shares one budget.
  3. Its port 2 is the carrier board's hub: the four USB-A ports (1-2).
  4. Our four cameras, each run by the uvcvideo driver: TopLeft on hub port 1, TopRight on 3, the two global-shutter cameras on 2 and 4.
  5. Port 3 is the Wi-Fi card's Bluetooth (rtk_btusb), on the very same bus. The USB-C port (port 1) has nothing plugged in.

Power modes: what "Super" means

The same board can run cool and slow or hot and fast. NVIDIA calls these power modes, and you switch with one command, nvpmodel. The "Super" in the name comes from a 2024 software update that unlocked a faster mode on the same hardware: GPU 635 → 1020 MHz, CPU 1.5 → 1.7 GHz, memory 68 → 102 GB/s.

We use the fastest, MAXN SUPER (sudo nvpmodel -m 2), and then lock every clock at its maximum with jetson_clocks at every boot. A locked clock means the detector takes the same time on every frame, instead of waiting for the chip to speed up.

What we measured: going fanless

A sealed Jetson would be nice on a robot (no dust, no fan to fail). So we switched the fan off and watched. At full power the stock heatsink climbed from 42 to 85 °C in under 10 minutes and was still rising. The good news: a dead fan isn't fatal. The chip slowly climbs to its throttle point and slows down, instead of shutting off.

Now we're testing the stock heatsink with its fan sealed under an aluminium plate and thermal tape. FAN=off keeps the fan stopped. If the chip reaches 95 °C, the Jetson caps every camera at 60 fps until it's under 88 °C (spectrum-fan-guard and photonvision-57; the Match Ready page shows it). That's before the chip's own throttling at 99 °C.

Make sure "off" really means off

Our first plate tests seemed to level off at about 73 °C. Great news, we thought. But the fan wasn't really off: Linux's own thermal settings switch it on at 74 °C, and that's what held the temperature there. Now FAN=off takes the fan away from the kernel too. A test only proves what it really tested.

Power from the robot

A robot battery starts around 12–13 V and sags when motors pull hard, sometimes well below 10 V. Computers hate that. So the Jetson doesn't take battery power directly: a 15 V boost regulator turns whatever the battery gives into a steady 15 V for the DC jack.

On 2026-09-25 we tested it by lowering the input voltage on a bench supply: the Jetson kept running down to a 5 V input. That's far below any brownout the robot will see.

Five cameras at 122 fps take 11.1–11.2 W (facing bright ceiling lights, the hard case). While the robot is disabled, each camera processes only 30 frames a second instead of 122, so five cameras take 7.8 W instead of 9.7 W (photonvision-49). That's less heat in the queue.

If the supply does sag, the Jetson reports it: PhotonVision publishes a throttle reason (OVER-CURRENT, HIGH TEMP) on NetworkTables so robot code can raise an alert (chapter 16).

Jetson vs. a Raspberry Pi

Many FRC teams run PhotonVision on an Orange Pi 5 or a Raspberry Pi. They're cheaper, and they work well. The difference is where the work happens. Each dot below is one processing core, drawn to scale by count.

Being fair about it:

  • One Pi CPU core is not slower than one Jetson CPU core; it runs at a higher clock (2.4 GHz vs 1.7 GHz). A single GPU core is much weaker than either. The GPU wins only on jobs it can split into thousands of identical pieces, and finding tags in a million pixels is one of those (chapter 07 shows why).
  • Stock PhotonVision finds AprilTags on the CPU on every coprocessor. Ours is a special version that sends that work to the Jetson's GPU (chapters 08–09).
  • The Jetson costs more and draws more power. A Pi is a great choice for one or two cameras at moderate frame rates. We wanted four cameras at 120 fps each at full resolution, with CPU to spare.
