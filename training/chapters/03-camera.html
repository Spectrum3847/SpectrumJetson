03 · Capturing light

Inside the camera: from light to data.

A camera is a machine that counts light. In about 8 milliseconds, ours turns the light bouncing off a tag into a million numbers, squeezes them into about 50 kilobytes, and sends them down a USB cable.

Our AprilTag cameras are $50 Thrifty Bot Thriftiest Cams. The lens focuses light from the tag onto an OV9281 sensor: 1,024,000 tiny light buckets that fill with electrons during the exposure, then get measured and turned into brightness numbers, 120 times a second. It's monochrome, so every pixel gets all the light, and global shutter, so every pixel captures at the same instant and a moving tag isn't skewed. A chip in the camera squeezes each 1 MB frame into a JPEG of about 50 KB so it fits over USB 2.0.

The journey of light

Follow the light from a tag, through the camera, to the USB cable. Scroll through each step.

1 · Light

Light bounces off the tag

The field lights shine on the tag. The white squares bounce most of that light back; the black squares soak most of it up. Every point on the tag sends light out in all directions, and a tiny bit of it heads toward our camera.

2 · Lens

The lens brings it to a focus

Light from one point on the tag spreads out, so without a lens it would land everywhere and make a blur. The lens bends it back together, so all the light from one point on the tag lands on one point of the sensor. The image comes out upside down (the software doesn't care).

Our lens has a short 2.1 mm focal length, which gives a wide 82° view. It's made of 3 elements, 2 glass and 1 plastic, that work together to keep the image sharp.

3 · IR-cut filter

Infrared is filtered out

Silicon sensors can see near-infrared light, which your eyes can't. The filter lets through at least 88% of visible light (430–620 nm) and less than 5% of infrared (700–1100 nm).

Why block it? The lens is designed to focus visible light, and infrared comes to a focus at a slightly different distance, which would soften the image. Many materials also reflect infrared very differently from how they look. Blocking it keeps tag edges crisp and the picture predictable.

4 · Photodiodes

Photons become electrons

The sensor is a grid of 1,024,000 tiny light-sensitive photodiodes, each only 3 micrometers wide. When a photon hits one, it knocks loose an electron, which is stored in the pixel.

Think of each pixel as a bucket in the rain. While the exposure lasts, photons are the rain. Pixels looking at a white square fill up; pixels looking at a black square stay nearly empty.

5 · Readout

Measure every bucket

When the exposure ends, every pixel stops collecting at the same instant (that's what global shutter means). Then the sensor reads the buckets out, row by row. An ADC (analog-to-digital converter) measures each bucket's charge and turns it into a number.

The OV9281 measures each pixel with 10 bits (0–1023). The pictures we get over USB use 8 bits: 0 is black, 255 is white.

6 · A grid of numbers

An image is just numbers

That's all a digital image is: a grid of brightness numbers. These are real pixel values from our own TopLeft Thriftiest Cam, around the top-left corner of tag 3. You can see the black border (low numbers), the white edge (high numbers), and the soft, in-between values right at the edge.

Our full frame is 1280 × 800 of these: 1,024,000 bytes, about 1 MB.

7 · JPEG

Squeeze it 20 times smaller

1 MB × 120 frames a second is 123 MB/s, about 980 megabits a second. USB 2.0 only carries 480 in total, shared by every camera. So a chip in the camera compresses each frame into a JPEG before sending it.

JPEG cuts the image into 8 × 8 blocks and describes each block as a mix of simple patterns, keeping the strong ones and dropping fine detail you'd barely notice. Real frames from our cameras come out at 49.7–60.4 KB; the one in this chapter is 52,801 bytes.

8 · USB

Down the cable, 125 µs at a time

USB 2.0 splits time into 125-microsecond slices called microframes, 8,000 a second. Our camera driver gives each camera a fixed number of bytes in every slice (chapter 10). At our setting, a 50 KB frame takes about 4.9 ms to cross, then the Jetson decodes it and looks for tags.

The camera sends this stream as MJPEG: a series of separate JPEG pictures, one per frame.

Global shutter vs rolling shutter

Most phone and webcam sensors use a rolling shutter: they start and stop collecting light one row at a time, top to bottom. The whole scan takes a few milliseconds. If something moves during that scan, the top of the picture sees it in one place and the bottom sees it somewhere else.

Our OV9281 has a global shutter: every pixel starts and stops at exactly the same moment. A moving object may still blur if the exposure is long (chapter 04), but it won't be bent or skewed.

For AprilTags this matters a lot. On a spinning or driving robot, a rolling shutter turns the square tag into a slanted shape with its corners in the wrong places, so the pose calculated from them is wrong.

Why black-and-white?

A photodiode only counts photons; it can't tell what color they are. A color sensor puts a tiny color filter over every pixel, in a pattern called a Bayer filter: half the pixels green, a quarter red, a quarter blue. Each pixel only sees its one color, and the camera guesses the other two from the neighbors ("demosaicing").

That costs a color camera twice:

  • Less light. Each filter throws away roughly two-thirds of the light, so each pixel collects about a third as much. To get the same brightness you need a longer exposure (more blur) or more gain (more noise).
  • Softer edges. Guessing the missing colors smears detail across neighboring pixels, and sharp edges pick up false color fringes.

AprilTags are black and white. Color adds nothing, so a mono sensor gets all the light and all the sharpness.

JPEG: small files, small costs

JPEG doesn't store pixels. It cuts the frame into 8 × 8 blocks and stores each block as a recipe: "this much average brightness, plus this much of a gentle left-to-right fade, plus a little of this stripe pattern..." Fine patterns get rounded off first. The quality setting decides how hard it rounds. Try it on a real frame.

What we measured

Our cameras choose their own JPEG quality. At 120 fps, one of our Thriftiest Cams averaged 47.5 KB per frame at its normal USB allocation (1280 bytes per microframe). When we halved that allocation, it kept 120 fps and compressed harder instead: 40.8 KB per frame. That may cost some tag range and precision, which we still have to measure. Blocky JPEG edges are also part of why our calibrations can't get below about 0.8 px of error (chapter 11).

Wide lens or narrow lens?

The lens decides the trade-off between seeing a lot and seeing far. The sensor always has 1280 pixels across. A wide lens spreads them over a wide view, so each tag gets fewer pixels. A narrow (longer focal length) lens packs them into a small view: tags look bigger and can be read from farther away, but you see fewer of them and lose them when the robot turns.

The focal length in pixels is the lens's focal length divided by the pixel size. For ours: 2.1 mm ÷ 0.003 mm ≈ 700 px. Our calibration measured 737 px, which is how our lens really behaves: about 2.2 mm (the catalog's 2.1 mm is a nominal value).

Change the lens, redo the calibration

The lens is part of the camera's calibration (chapter 11). Swap it, or even bump its focus, and the old calibration is wrong.

Why USB, and why UVC?

Our cameras speak UVC, the USB Video Class: a standard that says how any USB camera describes itself and sends video. Linux has one driver for all of them, uvcvideo, so a UVC camera works the moment it's plugged in, with no special software. The same standard defines the camera's settings, like exposure and brightness (chapter 04). Chapter 06 looks at the driver, and the patch we made to it.

Focus, then lock it

An M12 lens screws into its holder. Turning it moves the glass closer to or farther from the sensor, which changes what distance is sharp. A soft image blurs tag edges, lowers the decision margin, and makes corners jitter.

Our build adds a Focus card to the Camera page (photonvision-34). It scores sharpness using the "variance of the Laplacian": it measures how strongly brightness changes from each pixel to its neighbors. Sharp edges give big changes; blur smooths them away. It scores a 3 × 3 grid, so you can also catch a tilted lens.

  1. Point the camera at something detailed that stays still, at the distance you care about most.
  2. Press Reset, turn the lens slowly past the sharpest point, then back until the center reads 100%.
  3. Check the corners. Far below 100% means a tilted lens or a soft corner.
  4. Lock the lens with glue or silicone, then recalibrate. Vibration on a robot can turn a loose lens and spoil the calibration.
