04 · Capturing light

Camera settings: exposure, brightness, contrast, gain and friends.

A camera has a handful of knobs that decide what every frame looks like. Most of them trade one problem for another. The most important one, by far, is exposure, and the lesson of this chapter fits in four words: short exposure on a robot.

Exposure is how long each pixel collects light. On a moving robot, everything smears while the shutter is open: spinning at 3 rad/s smears a tag about 11 px at 5 ms but 44 px at 20 ms. Shorter exposure is darker, which lowers the decision margin (how confidently each tag is read), so we run 5 ms with a decision margin cutoff of 15.

USB cameras set exposure in units of 100 µs, so our setting of 50 means 5 ms. Exposure also limits the frame rate: at 120 fps a new frame starts every 8.3 ms, so the exposure has to fit inside that. Try it in the simulator: raise the exposure while the robot spins.

Why short exposure wins

While the shutter is open, a moving robot keeps moving, so every point of the tag gets smeared along its path. How far? For a robot turning in place:

smear (px) ≈ 737 px × spin (rad/s) × exposure (s)

At 3 rad/s that's about 11 px at 5 ms but 44 px at 20 ms. A tag square at a few meters is only 5–10 px wide, so 44 px of smear wipes out the pattern completely, and even when the tag is still found, its corners are fuzzy and the pose jitters.

The catch: shorter exposure means less light, a darker image, and a lower decision margin. That's the trade we tuned: 5 ms is short enough to keep blur small, bright enough for good margins under typical field lights, and we lowered the decision margin cutoff to 15 to match.

The standing-still trap

Auto exposure, or tuning by eye with the robot sitting still, always picks a long exposure: the picture looks bright and clean. It falls apart the moment the robot moves. Tune for the moving robot, not the pretty picture.

Calibration is the one exception. The board is held still, so a long exposure (about 15 ms) is fine there. Just don't copy it into the vision pipelines.

The exposure units trap

USB cameras follow the UVC standard, which counts exposure in units of 100 microseconds. So an exposure setting of 295 means 29.5 milliseconds, not 0.3 ms. Stock PhotonVision didn't say so, and 295 looked like a small number.

A frame can't be shorter than its own exposure, so the exposure caps the frame rate: at most 1000 ÷ exposure-in-ms frames per second. At 120 fps, a new frame starts every 8.3 ms. An exposure of 29.5 ms means at most 34 frames a second, no matter how fast everything else is.

What we found

Our starting point was exposure 295: 33 fps and 44 ms latency, while the GPU sat idle. We only found the cause by reading the camera's controls directly with v4l2-ctl. Dropping to 83 (8.3 ms) let the camera deliver 120 fps; after that, PhotonVision itself was the limit (chapter 10). Our build now shows milliseconds on the slider, e.g. "Exposure (5.0 ms)" (photonvision-10).

Flickering lights

Most indoor lights run on mains power, which alternates 60 times a second. Many lights get brighter and dimmer on every half of the cycle: 120 times a second, once every 8.3 ms. Your eyes average it out. A camera with a short exposure doesn't.

If the exposure is a whole number of flicker cycles (8.3 ms, 16.7 ms…), every frame collects exactly the same amount of light. Otherwise, each frame catches a different slice of the flicker and the brightness pulses from frame to frame. Tags near the decision margin cutoff then flicker in and out of detection.

Our cameras run at about 122 fps, just off the lights' 120 Hz. So neighboring frames catch almost the same slice of the flicker, and the brightness drifts slowly up and down, a couple of times a second, instead of jumping from frame to frame. That's why it's worth looking at the total range over a few seconds, not only the change between neighboring frames; flicker-check reports both.

What we measured

We wrote tests/flicker-check: it records 10 s of video and reports each camera's frame-to-frame brightness change. In our shop, at 5 ms, it measured 0.6% on average, 1% at most: no flicker under our shop LEDs.

Event lights are different, so run it again there. If frames pulse by several percent, go back to 83 (8.3 ms).

The hidden controls

Stock PhotonVision doesn't show contrast, gamma, sharpness or backlight compensation. Ours does (photonvision-28), with the camera's own ranges: contrast 0–95 (default 32), gamma 100–300 (150), sharpness 1–10 (5). Leave them at the defaults unless you're testing; sharpness is the one worth trying lower, because sharpening can halo tag edges.
