02 · The big picture

AprilTags: why a black-and-white square is special.

An AprilTag looks like a chunky QR code. It's really a carefully designed landmark: easy to spot from across the field, hard to mistake for anything else, and shaped so a computer can tell exactly where it is in 3D.

An AprilTag is a printed black-and-white square that a computer can find in a camera picture fast, identify by its ID, and pinpoint by its four corners. FRC uses the tag36h11 family: a 6.5 inch black square with a 6 × 6 grid of squares inside that spells the ID. Unlike a QR code, a tag has only 36 big squares, so it stays readable from about 6 m away with our camera (you need roughly 2 pixels per square). A landmark a computer can read

An AprilTag is a printed square pattern. A program can find it in a camera picture very quickly, read its ID number, and work out exactly where its four corners are.

FRC uses the tag36h11 family. Each tag is a 6.5 inch (165.1 mm) black square on a white plate. Inside the black border is a 6 × 6 grid of black and white squares that spells out the tag's ID.

The tags are bolted to the field elements (the hubs, trenches, towers and outposts) at published positions. FIRST gives every team a field layout file that lists each tag's x, y and height, and which way it faces. So when the robot sees tag 26, it knows exactly where tag 26 is. Tap a tag on the map below to see its entry.

The big idea

If you know where a landmark is, and you can measure where it appears in your camera, you can work backwards to where you are. That's the whole trick of this course, done 120 times a second.

Our layout

Events use either a welded field or an AndyMark field, and the tags sit a few centimeters differently on each. Our robot uses the 2026 Rebuilt AndyMark layout, and the robot code must use the same file as PhotonVision.

Why not just use a QR code?

A QR code is built to hold a lot of data (a whole web address) and to be read by a phone held a few inches away. A robot has the opposite problem. It needs to read a tiny, tilted, blurry tag from across the field, dozens of times a second, and it only needs one small number. So an AprilTag makes different trade-offs:

  • Few, big bits. Only 36 data squares, so each one is big. Big squares survive being far away, low-resolution and seen at a steep angle.
  • Fast to find. A thick black square border against a white edge is easy to spot. The detector looks for dark four-sided shapes and ignores everything else.
  • Four corners you can measure. The tag's size is known exactly, so where its 4 corners land in the image tells you its distance and angle: a full 3D pose.
  • Hard to misread. The codes are chosen so that any two differ in at least 11 squares. Random patterns, or a tag with a few squares misread, don't turn into the wrong ID.

How far away can you read it?

A camera doesn't see a tag, it sees pixels. The farther away the tag, the fewer pixels land on it. Our camera has 1280 pixels across an 82° view, which works out to a focal length of about 737 pixels (we measured it in calibration, chapter 11). That gives a simple rule:

pixels across the tag ≈ 737 × 0.1651 m ÷ distance

The rule of thumb: about 2 pixels per square

To tell whether a square is black or white, the detector samples the middle of it. If a square is only 1 pixel wide, that pixel usually straddles two squares and comes out gray, and the code turns to mush. With about 2 or more pixels per square, most squares have a "clean" pixel in the middle.

An AprilTag has 8 squares across its black part (6 data + 2 border), so each is 20.6 mm. A same-size QR code with 25 modules has squares of only 6.6 mm. That's why the tag stays readable about 3 times farther away.

What we measured

We took 40 real frames of tag 3, shrank them to smaller and smaller sizes, re-compressed them with the camera's own JPEG settings, and ran our real detector on them. The tag was found in 85% of frames at 20 pixels wide (about 6 m face-on), 52% at 18 px, and only 2% at 14 px.

20 px is 2.5 pixels per square, so the rule of thumb holds. Our GPU detector looks for tag outlines on a half-size image to save time, which costs some range: searching at full size found 98% at 12 px (about 10 m). Real far-away tags are blurrier than shrunk ones, so real ranges are a bit shorter.

36h11: codes that can't be confused

The name tag36h11 is a spec sheet: 36 data bits, and a minimum Hamming distance of 11. Hamming distance just means "how many squares are different". Any two tags in the family differ in at least 11 of their 36 squares.

Why does that matter? A bit can be misread: glare on one square, a shadow on another. If two tags differed in only one square, a single mistake would turn tag 7 into tag 8, and the robot would think it's somewhere else entirely. With 11 squares between every pair of codes, a few mistakes leave the pattern much closer to the right tag than to any other one, or close to no tag at all.

Rotation is built in. A tag can appear sideways or upside down. The detector reads the grid, then compares it against every code in all four rotations. The family was chosen so each code is at least 11 squares away from every rotation of every other code, and from its own rotations too. So a match tells you both the ID and which way up the tag is, and that's how the detector knows which corner is which.

Analogy

Imagine phone numbers chosen so any two differ in at least 5 of their 10 digits. Misdial one digit and you won't reach a stranger; the number you dialed doesn't belong to anybody, and you can tell which number you meant. The full tag36h11 family has only 587 codes out of 68 billion possible 36-bit patterns, which is what buys that safety.

A real misread

This happens on real fields. In this frame from the 2024 field, the AprilTag library read tag 7 perfectly, but read tag 6 with 2 wrong squares (and a low decision margin of 32.5). The library was allowed to fix up to 2, so it still reported tag 6.

Our system is stricter. Our detector library repairs at most 1 square (apriltag_detector_add_family_bits(td, family, 1) in detector/GpuDetectorJNI.cc), and PhotonVision keeps only tags read with 0 wrong squares (its hamming setting). We'd simply skip tag 6 in this frame and use the next one, 8 ms later. Missing one frame is cheap; trusting a bad reading is not.

How the detector finds a tag, step by step

Here's the real process, run live in your browser on a real frame from a robot camera on the 2024 field. Scroll through it. Every image is computed by the step it describes, except the line fitting in step 4, where we use the corners the real AprilTag library found.

Step 1 · Grayscale frame

Start with brightness only

The detector only cares how bright each pixel is, 0 (black) to 255 (white). Our cameras are black-and-white already, so there's no color to throw away (this camera was a color one, so we converted it).

Two tags sit on the 2024 Speaker. Everything else, the logos, the scoreboard, the lights and the diamond plate, is stuff the detector has to ignore.

Step 2 · Adaptive threshold GPU

Every pixel becomes black or white

First the frame is shrunk to half size, which is plenty for finding outlines and 4 times less work. One fixed cut-off ("brighter than 128 is white") fails when the lighting is uneven, so the image is cut into 4 × 4 pixel tiles. Each tile finds its darkest and brightest pixel (also looking at its neighbors), and its pixels are called white if they're above the halfway point.

Tiles where dark and bright are too close together, like a plain wall, can't hold a tag edge. They're marked "skip" (purple). Our detector sets this "too close" limit to 20 brightness levels, which makes it about 30–40% faster.

Step 3 · Connected blobs GPU

Group touching pixels into blobs

Neighboring pixels of the same color are joined into one blob, using a method called union-find. Each color here is a separate blob. A tag's black border becomes one big ring-shaped blob, with the white area around it as another.

On the GPU, thousands of threads each look at a few pixels at once and merge their groups.

Step 4 · Edges and quads GPU

Find black–white boundaries, fit four lines

Wherever a black blob touches a white blob, the boundary pixels are collected (green). The detector then fits straight lines through each boundary and keeps shapes made of exactly four good lines: quads. Most boundaries (the clutter) don't make a clean quad and are thrown out.

We didn't rebuild the line fitting here: the amber quads are the ones the real AprilTag library found in this frame.

Step 5 · Sample the grid CPU

Read the squares through the tilt

Now the detector goes back to the full-size image. A tilted tag looks like a squashed four-sided shape. Using its four corners, the detector works out where each of the 8 × 8 squares should be (a homography) and samples the brightness at each square's center. Green dots read white, red dots read black.

Step 6 · Decode CPU

Match against the code list

The 36 samples become 36 bits, compared against every code in all 4 rotations. An exact match gives the ID, which way up the tag is, and the four corners in order (numbered from the tag's own top-left). The labels show what this page's decoder read from the pixels: tags 4 and 3, 0 wrong squares, and the decision margin (explained below).

Step 7 · Pose

Four corners become a 3D position

Four corners, a known tag size, and the camera's calibration are enough to solve for the tag's position and angle in 3D (chapter 12). The axes show each tag's pose (approximate here, since we don't have this camera's calibration): x, y, and z pointing out of the tag.

Steps 2–4 do the same small job for every pixel, so our detector (by Austin Schuh of FRC 971) runs them on the Jetson's GPU, thousands at once; the whole detection takes about 2 ms a frame. Chapter 08 shows how.

The detector's real settings
  • Half-size quad search. The GPU detector finds tag outlines on a half-size (640 × 400) copy of the image, then refines the corners and reads the bits at full size. The half size is hard-wired in the code (CHECK_EQ(quad_decimate, 2)), which is why our range test above found more range when the image was searched at full size.
  • min_white_black_diff = 20. The "skip" limit from step 2. AprilTag's default is 5; Austin's current code moved to 20 for speed. In our A/B test it made the detector 30–40% faster (1.7 ms instead of 2.4–3.0 ms per frame) with identical detections.
  • max_line_fit_mse = 10 (AprilTag's default): how wobbly an edge can be and still count as a straight side. Upstream PhotonVision lowered its CPU detector to 2.5 so tags cut off at the image edge are rejected; we plan to test that before changing it.
  • Threads. The CPU part (edge refinement and decoding) uses a pool of 6 worker threads per camera.

Decision margin: how sure is the reading?

Reading a square isn't a yes-or-no thing to the camera. It measures a brightness, and the detector decides which side of a threshold it's on. A square that reads 240 when the threshold is 128 is clearly white. One that reads 131 is barely white.

The decision margin puts a number on that. For one tag, the detector works out:

  • how far, on average, the white squares are above the threshold,
  • how far, on average, the black squares are below it,

and reports the smaller of the two. A big margin means strong contrast between the tag's black and white. A small margin means a dim, washed-out or blurry tag, or a random pattern that only looks like one.

PhotonVision throws away tags below a cutoff (35 by default). Try the lab: dim the light and watch the margin fall.

What we found

On our bench the margin tracked exposure: about 44 at 3 ms, about 118 at 8.3 ms. With the default cutoff of 35, short exposures sat right at the edge and tags flickered in and out, even though the detector found them in every frame. So we tune exposure and cutoff together: 5 ms with a cutoff of 15 (chapter 04). A false tag would still need a perfect 36h11 match.

The exact decision-margin formula

In the AprilTag library (quad_decode in apriltag.c), the detector first fits two brightness models across the tag, one from its black border and one from the white ring around it: intensity(x, y) = C0·x + C1·y + C2. That handles a tag that's brighter on one side. Each square's threshold is halfway between the two models at that spot, and each sample becomes v = sample − threshold.

Positive v adds to white_score, negative adds −v to black_score, and the margin is min(white_score / white_count, black_score / black_count), in brightness levels (0–255). The lab uses the same formula with one flat threshold.

Tag families, and why FRC uses 36h11

AprilTag families trade size against safety. tag16h5 has 16 bits and distance 5: bigger squares, but only 30 codes, and random patterns match it much more easily. FRC used it in 2023, and teams saw false detections, so FRC switched to tag36h11 in 2024. Its 11-bit distance makes false matches extremely rare, and 587 codes is plenty. The cost is smaller squares (8 across instead of 6), so slightly less range for the same printed size.

AprilTag 3 also added "recursive" families like tagStandard41h12 and tagCircle21h7, which use space inside and outside the border differently. FRC doesn't use them.
