11 · The math of seeing

Camera calibration: teaching the robot its lens.

A camera gives you pixels. The robot needs angles and distances. To turn one into the other you have to know exactly what the lens does, down to a fraction of a pixel. Measuring that is called calibration.

Why a camera needs calibrating

Say the camera sees a tag's corner at pixel 900 across. Which direction is that? It depends on the lens. A wide lens squeezes a big slice of the world onto the sensor, so pixel 900 might be 25° to the right. A narrower lens might put the same pixel at 15°.

Even two cameras of the same model aren't identical. The lens sits a little off-center, the glass bends light slightly differently, and the sensor is glued in at a tiny angle. Those differences are small, but a robot doing geometry with a tag 5 m away feels every one of them.

Calibration measures your camera's exact lens: how strongly it magnifies, where its true center is, and how it bends straight lines. We call these numbers the camera's intrinsics, because they're about the inside of the camera.

The pinhole camera

The model every vision system starts from is the pinhole camera: a dark box with a tiny hole. Light from a point in the world travels in a straight line through the hole and lands on the sensor. Each point in the world lands on exactly one spot.

Two kinds of numbers describe where it lands. The focal length is how far the sensor sits behind the hole: farther means more zoomed in. The principal point is the pixel directly behind the hole, the image's true center. Computer vision measures both in pixels, so they plug straight into the math.

The image plane is drawn in front of the pinhole, the usual trick in computer vision. On the real sensor, behind the hole, the picture is upside down; the math is the same. The sensor's pixels are 3 µm wide, so a focal length of 737 px is about 2.2 mm.

Four numbers, one matrix

Divide by the distance Z (things farther away look smaller), scale by the focal length, and shift by the principal point. That's the whole pinhole model:

u = fx · X / Z + cx
v = fy · Y / Z + cy

Vision code packs the four numbers into a 3×3 grid called the intrinsic matrix, K. You'll see it in PhotonVision's calibration files and in PhotonLib as camera.getCameraMatrix().

fx and fy are nearly equal because the pixels are square. The principal point is not exactly the center of the image (640, 400). Our two cameras' lenses sit tens of pixels off-center, and each one differently.

Lenses bend straight lines

A real lens isn't a pinhole. Its curved glass bends light a little more near the edges than in the middle. Straight lines in the world come out curved in the picture: bulging outward (barrel distortion, common in wide lenses) or pinched inward (pincushion).

Calibration measures this bending too, as a handful of distortion coefficients. Radial ones (k1, k2, k3…) bend points toward or away from the center, more the farther out they are. Tangential ones (p1, p2) handle a lens that's slightly tilted. Once you know them, you can undo the bending and get the straight-line pinhole picture back.

What we fixed: the lens model was cut short

PhotonVision's calibration produces OpenCV's 8-coefficient "rational" lens model: k1 k2 p1 p2 k3 k4 k5 k6. The CUDA detector uses the lens model while it refines each tag's corners: it straightens the tag's edges, fits the lines, then bends the corners back. But the 4143 fork passed it only the first 5 numbers. The missing three matter most near the edges of the image, so corners there came out slightly wrong. Our patch photonvision-04 adds a setparams8 call that passes all 8, and the log now says "(8 dist coeffs)" for each camera.

The full lens model, and where it gives up

For a point at normalized position (x, y) = (X/Z, Y/Z) with r² = x² + y², OpenCV's rational model moves it to:

x' = x · (1 + k1r² + k2r⁴ + k3r⁶) / (1 + k4r² + k5r⁴ + k6r⁶) + 2p1xy + p2(r² + 2x²)
y' = y · (same ratio) + p1(r² + 2y²) + 2p2xy
u = fx · x' + cx,   v = fy · y' + cy

The 5-coefficient model is the same with k4 = k5 = k6 = 0, so dropping the last three turns the fraction's bottom line into 1. That's the bug above.

These formulas are just a curve fitted to the snapshots, and a fitted curve can misbehave where there was no data. The chart plots our TopRight model: how far from the center a point lands (distorted) against how far it should be (ideal). Past about 47° off-axis, the curve turns back, so two different directions land on the same pixel. We found this building field calibration (tools/fieldcal): 4% of TopRight's image and 1% of TopLeft's (which turns back at 49°) can't be modeled. The tool ignores those areas. A calibration with board views right into the corners would fix it.

Inside and outside the camera

The lens numbers are only half of what the robot needs. It also has to know where the camera is bolted on and which way it points. Those are the extrinsics: a position (x, y, z) and a rotation (roll, pitch, yaw), usually relative to the center of the robot on the floor. In WPILib robot code it's a Transform3d called robotToCamera.

The calibration board

To measure a lens, you show it something whose shape you know perfectly: a flat board printed with a pattern. Ours is a ChArUco board, a chessboard with a small ArUco marker printed in each white square.

  • 12 × 9 squares, each 30 mm, with 22 mm markers from the 5×5 dictionary.
  • That gives 88 inner corners (11 × 8), where four squares meet. Corners are what calibration measures: they can be found to a fraction of a pixel.
  • The markers give each corner a name. Every marker's pattern is different, so seeing one tells the software exactly which squares are around it.

A plain chessboard has a problem: every corner looks the same. The software can only tell which corner is which by counting from the edges, so the whole board has to be in view. That makes it very hard to get corners near the edges of the image, exactly where the lens distorts most.

What we found: 12 × 9, not 9 × 12

Our board is labeled "9x12". Entered that way in PhotonVision, every calibration failed with "Negative corner in reprojection error calc". We wrote tests/charuco-board-check/check_board.py, which tries all four combinations of width, height and marker layout on a live camera frame. Only width 12, height 9 found corners: 78 of 88, with 51 of 54 markers read. Our PhotonVision build (photonvision-23) now starts with our board's settings, in millimeters.

From snapshots to lens numbers

Calibrating means taking many snapshots of the board, held at different places, distances and angles. For each snapshot the software finds the corners. Then it asks one big question: which lens numbers, and which board positions, would make a pinhole camera with this distortion see the corners exactly where they were found?

No answer fits perfectly, because every corner is measured with a little noise. So it finds the numbers that fit best, by least squares: guess, measure how far every predicted corner is from its detected spot, nudge all the numbers to shrink those misses, and repeat. The average miss that's left is the reprojection error.

The trap in that lab

With every snapshot in the middle, the reprojection error looks great, about the same as a good calibration. But the lens error near the edges is tens of pixels, because the solver never saw a corner there and its curve can do anything outside the data. A low reprojection error only means the model fits the snapshots you took. Coverage is what makes it right everywhere else.

Reading the result

Under about 0.5 px is ideal, and under 1 px is fine for FRC. We couldn't get below 0.5 px with a hand-held board, and that's okay: the remaining ~0.8–0.9 px is mostly noise from the camera's JPEG compression.

Outliers are the better warning sign. The solver throws out corners that don't fit at all. Only 3–4% were thrown out in our good calibrations. Our second try threw out 42%, because many snapshots had the board mostly outside the frame. For edge coverage, keep most of the board in the image and just touch the edge.

We check every calibration with tests/calibration-check/check_calibration.py, which reads the saved calibration and reports the error, the outliers and the coverage.

How the solver really works

PhotonVision calibrates with mrcal. Like the lab, it minimizes the sum of squared reprojection errors over all the unknowns at once: the lens numbers and a 6-number pose for every snapshot. The lab uses Levenberg–Marquardt, a least-squares method that blends careful small steps with bold Gauss–Newton jumps.

With 100 snapshots that's 612 unknowns, but most of the grid of partial derivatives is zero: a snapshot's pose only affects its own corners. The trick that keeps it fast is the Schur complement. Each snapshot's pose is solved away, leaving a small 12×12 system for the lens alone.

A real calibration also needs a first guess. OpenCV gets one from Zhang's method: each flat board view gives a homography, and a few homographies pin down the focal length and center. The lab cheats and starts near each board's true pose.

Why tilt matters: a flat board straight on can't tell a longer focal length from a board that's farther away; both make it look bigger. Tilted views break the tie, because perspective changes the board's shape differently.

Field calibration: the real field, and every mount

A lens calibration is done once, on the bench. Two other numbers can be wrong at an event: where the tags really are (a field element built a couple of centimeters off) and where each camera sits on the robot (CAD says one thing, the real mount another). Pitch matters most: a 1° pitch error puts a tag 4 m away off by about 7 cm.

Our PhotonVision has a Field Calibration page (photonvision-30) that measures both with the robot's own cameras, during the event's field calibration time.

  2. Record the spots. Push the robot by hand to 10–20 spots on flat carpet and hold it still about 2 s at each, until the page beeps. A map shows which tags need more views.
  3. Solve. The Jetson replays the recording through the same 971 GPU detector, then one least-squares solve (bundle adjustment) fits every tag's pose, every spot's robot pose and every camera's mount. About a minute.
  4. Results. Each tag marked as drawn or moved, each mount compared with robot code's, and a button to load the corrected layout (with Undo).
What we built: tested, not yet on a real field

So far the solver has been tested on synthetic data, including whole rendered recordings with the official tag images, our lens calibration, blur, noise and JPEG. On those it found tags to a median of 0.2–0.7 cm, moved tags of 15 and 25 cm to within about 1 cm, and mounts to about 1 cm or better. It also caught both mistakes planted in the test's CAD file: one camera's pitch off by 1.5°, another's height off by 1 cm. Next is a shop test on our half field. We expect about 1 cm in real life, limited by the lens calibration and the floor.

A quicker check: the camera mount estimate

With the robot level on the floor and 2+ tags in view, the Targets tab's Camera mount estimate (photonvision-17) shows the camera's height, pitch and roll, averaged over the last 100 samples. While the robot is level, the camera's height, pitch and roll on the field are its mount's, wherever the robot is. The numbers use robotToCamera's signs, so they go straight into robot code, and robot code can compare them with its configured mount to catch a bumped camera.
