01 · The big picture

Why robots need vision.

A robot that knows exactly where it is on the field can drive itself, aim at a goal and line up in a fraction of a second. Its wheels can tell it roughly where it is. A camera tells it for sure.

Wheels slowly lie

Every FRC robot keeps a running guess of its position called odometry: it counts how far each wheel turned and which way the gyro says it's facing, then adds it all up.

The trouble is that wheels slip. Push against another robot, bump over a field element, or spin quickly, and the wheel counts stop matching how far the robot really moved. Each small error adds to the last one. After a minute of hard driving, odometry can be off by more than a robot length.

Vision fixes the drift. Around the field are AprilTags, markers at known positions. When a camera sees one, the robot can work out exactly where it must be standing to see the tag that way, and correct its guess.

Meet the hardware

The robot's main computer is the SystemCore, which runs our robot code. Finding AprilTags 120 times a second on several cameras is too much work to add on top, so we give it to a second computer, a coprocessor: an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano Super.

The Jetson runs PhotonVision, free vision software many FRC teams use. Ours is a special version that sends the hardest work to the Jetson's GPU, using a detector written by Austin Schuh of FRC 971. We fixed bugs, made it faster and added match-day safety features, all covered later.

The photo on the right is our first bench test: the Jetson (the black board with the fan), one Thriftiest Cam, and a laptop running PhotonVision's web dashboard. The video shows the system tracking a tag at 121–123 frames per second. The colored lines are its guess of the tag's 3D position and angle, updated every frame.
