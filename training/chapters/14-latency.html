14 · The math of seeing

Latency: why "when" matters as much as "where."

Every vision answer describes the past. By the time the robot hears "you're here," it has already moved on. The trick isn't to make the delay zero. It's to know exactly how old each answer is.

Latency is the delay between something happening and finding out about it, like thunder arriving after the lightning. A camera frame shows where the tag was when its light hit the sensor. Then the exposure, USB, decoding, the GPU and the network add up to about 15 ms before the answer is ready for the robot.

A fast robot moves a lot in 15 ms: at 4 m/s that's 6 cm, and spinning is worse. So every frame carries a timestamp, the middle of its exposure, converted to the robot's clock by time sync. Robot code passes it to addVisionMeasurement(pose, timestamp), which applies the correction at that moment in the past and replays the wheels' movement since. Try both ways:

News is always a little old

Latency is the delay between something happening and you finding out about it. You've felt it:

  • Lightning and thunder. The flash reaches you almost instantly. The sound crawls along at about 343 m/s, so it takes about 3 seconds per kilometer. The thunder tells you about something that already happened.
  • A laggy video call. Your friend's face is a few hundred milliseconds behind reality, so you keep talking over each other.

A camera works the same way. A frame is a photo of where the tag was when the light hit the sensor. Everything after that (sending, decoding, finding the tag, doing the math, sending the answer) adds delay. When the answer reaches the robot, it's news about the past.

What we measured

PhotonVision showed 13–14 ms of latency after our fixes, down from 44 ms. How we got there (exposure, decode, USB caps) is the story of chapter 10.

Where was the robot when the picture was taken?

A robot that's driving fast moves a lot in 15 ms. If the robot code pretends a vision answer describes right now, it pulls its position estimate back toward where it used to be.

Driving: 4 m/s × 0.015 s = 6 cm of error.
Spinning is sneakier. At 3 rad/s (about half a turn per second), 15 ms is 0.045 rad ≈ 2.6°. A 2.6° heading mistake, seen from a tag 4 m away, puts the robot about 18 cm off (4 m × 0.045).

Try it in the lab. The field view is to scale, so the error is tiny there. The magnifier zooms in on the robot so you can see it.

The fix: don't guess, timestamp

Every frame carries a timestamp: the moment its light was captured, on the robot's clock. Robot code hands the pose and that timestamp to WPILib's pose estimator. The estimator looks up where odometry said the robot was at that moment, applies the correction there, then replays the odometry since. Latency stops mattering (almost).

Which instant is "the moment of capture"?

That fix needs to know exactly when each frame was captured. This sounds easy until you look closely. A frame isn't taken in an instant. The sensor collects light for the whole exposure (5 ms on our cameras), and a moving tag smears across that window. The blur's middle is where the tag was at the middle of the exposure. So that's the honest timestamp.

But the Jetson can't see the exposure happen. The first thing it notices is the frame's first USB packet arriving. Linux's camera driver stamps the frame right then. By that moment the whole exposure is over, and the camera has also spent some time reading out the sensor and starting the JPEG.

What we did

Our patch photonvision-13 subtracts half the exposure from every frame's timestamp, so it marks the middle of the exposure; robot code shouldn't subtract anything itself. We checked whether the camera sends its own hardware timestamps (UVC has a way to): it doesn't. The camera's internal delay (readout and JPEG) is still to be measured on the robot, by spinning in front of a tag.

The code that moves the timestamp

From photonvision-13-mid-exposure-timestamps.patch, in USBFrameProvider. The exposure comes from the camera setting, which is in 100 µs units (50 → 5,000 µs). With auto exposure on, the exposure time is unknown, so only the camera delay is subtracted.

private long toMidExposureUs(long captureTimeUs) {
    if (captureTimeUs == 0) return 0;
    double exposureUs = settables.getExposureTimeUs();   // -1 with auto exposure
    if (exposureUs <= 0) return captureTimeUs - CAMERA_DELAY_US;
    long shift = (long) (exposureUs / 2) + CAMERA_DELAY_US;
    return captureTimeUs - shift;
}

Why fix it on the Jetson and not as a constant in robot code? The Jetson knows the exposure exactly, and the exposure changes whenever someone retunes a camera. The patch's own comment gives the stakes: 2.5 ms late while turning at 360°/s is about 1° of heading error.

Why the USB bandwidth cap doesn't hurt timestamps: the driver stamps a frame at its first packet, so a frame that takes 4.9 ms to cross USB instead of 2 ms gets the same stamp. The answer arrives ~2 ms later, but it's labeled just as accurately.

Two computers, two clocks

There's one more catch. The timestamp above is on the Jetson's clock. The robot's pose estimator runs on the SystemCore's clock. Each computer counts time from when it booted, so their clocks can be minutes apart. A timestamp is useless until both agree what time it is.

PhotonVision solves this with time sync. The robot runs a tiny time server, and the Jetson asks it "what time is it?" over and over, on UDP port 5810, in microseconds. Because the question and the answer both take time to travel, the Jetson measures the round trip and assumes the answer spent half of it on the way back. It's the same idea as NTP, the protocol that keeps your phone's clock right.

Clock values are made up; the method is the real one. On a wired robot network the round trip is well under a millisecond, so the offset is known to a small fraction of a millisecond.

Offset math, and why it can only be off by half the unevenness

The Jetson sends a ping with its own time t0. The robot answers with t0 and its own time t1. The Jetson receives the answer at t2 (its clock).

  • Round trip: RTT = t2 − t0
  • Robot time "right now" ≈ t1 + RTT / 2
  • Offset = t1 + RTT/2 − t2. Add it to any Jetson time to get robot time.

If the trip there took a and the trip back took b, the estimate is off by (a − b)/2. Nothing can fix that without a third clock, so short, even network paths (a wired Ethernet cable) matter. PhotonVision keeps pinging, and PhotonLib hands your robot code result timestamps already converted to the robot's clock. The result's metadata even includes timeSinceLastPong, so robot code can tell if sync has gone stale.

The packet format is in PhotonVision's time sync design doc. Our 2026 PhotonVision and the robot's 2027 PhotonLib use the same packet layout and timebase, which we checked in the source.

Two kinds of clock

⏱️

Monotonic clock: for timing

A stopwatch that starts at boot and only ever counts up, steadily. Nobody can set it, so it never jumps. Frame timestamps, time sync and Rewind frame times all use this clock (nt::Now in WPILib's code). This is a monotonic clock.

used for vision
📅

Wall clock: for dates

The calendar date and time of day. It can jump when someone sets it. The Jetson has no clock battery, so after every power cut it wakes up thinking it's 1970, until something tells it the date.

used for logs and file names
🔗

The robot sets the Jetson's date

At events there's no internet. So robot code publishes its date on /photonvision/clock/unixMs, and our patch photonvision-08 sets the Jetson's wall clock from it. That fixes log times and Rewind names. It doesn't touch vision timing, because setting the date doesn't move the monotonic clock.

How the pose estimator rewinds time

Now each result carries a timestamp on the robot's own clock, and the pose estimator can use it. WPILib's pose estimator keeps a short history of odometry poses (about the last 1.5 seconds). When a vision measurement arrives with a timestamp in the past:

  1. It finds the odometry pose at that timestamp.
  2. It blends the vision pose in there, trusting each by its standard deviations (a Kalman filter-style update).
  3. It re-applies every wheel movement since then on top of the corrected pose.

In Java it's one line: poseEstimator.addVisionMeasurement(pose, timestampSeconds). The whole trick lives in that second argument.

Using the timestamp: the gyro heading at capture time

The same idea works for anything that changes while a frame is in flight. Chapter 13's heading-constrained solves pair a frame's corners with the gyro's heading. But by the time robot code sees the corners, the robot has turned a little more, so pairing them with the heading now mixes two different moments.

The fix: remember the recent headings, and look up the one from the exact moment the frame was captured. PhotonLib does this for you. Every loop, robot code calls addHeadingData(timestamp, heading), which fills a 1-second TimeInterpolatableBuffer. The heading-based solvers then read the heading at the frame's capture timestamp, interpolating between the two nearest samples.

What changes for us

Our off-season robot used Limelights. Its notes say Limelight's MegaTag2 used whatever heading was pushed last, not the heading at the frame's time. That's why the robot code has a gate that checks the yaw rate over the last 0.3 s. With PhotonLib, the heading is looked up at the frame's capture time, so that latency error goes away. We'll keep a looser gate anyway, for motion blur.

Turret cameras

A camera on a turret moves relative to the robot, so its robotToCamera changes all the time. Same fix, same idea: before each estimate, set the camera's transform from the turret's angle at the result's timestamp. That gives the turret camera a heading-held solve too, which the default Limelight mode couldn't do.

Why frame rate matters too

Latency is how old an answer is when it arrives. But between frames the robot has nothing new, so the newest answer keeps getting older until the next one lands. On average the robot's freshest vision is latency + half a frame period old.

  • Fresher data: at 30 fps the gap between frames is 33 ms. At 120 fps it's 8.3 ms.
  • More samples: a robot loop runs every 20 ms. At 120 fps each loop gets 2–3 new measurements per camera to average, which cuts random noise.

One trap: PhotonVision's "Low Latency Mode" sounds perfect but halved our frame rate, so we run it off (chapter 10).
