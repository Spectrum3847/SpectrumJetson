13 · The math of seeing

Why the gyro helps.

Vision is great at "how far" and "which direction", and shakiest at "which way am I facing". The robot already has a sensor that nails exactly that. Put the two together and one tag becomes enough.

The gyro knows which way you face

A gyro (part of the robot's IMU) measures how fast the robot is turning, hundreds of times a second. Add up all those little turns and you get the robot's heading: which way it's pointing on the field.

It isn't perfect. Tiny errors add up, so a gyro slowly drifts. But the drift is small and slow, and it doesn't care about lighting, distance or how many tags are in view. Over a match, a good gyro's heading is steadier than any single-tag vision heading.

Vision is the opposite. From chapter 12: a single tag's distance and direction are fairly solid, but its angle comes from the tiny squash of a small square. That's the weakest clue, and when it's wrong, the ambiguity flip can turn it into a disaster.

Small angle, big miss: the lever arm

Why does a small heading error matter so much? Vision finds the robot by walking back from the tag. If the angle is off, that whole walk swings around the tag like a door on a hinge, and the farther the tag, the longer the door. The sideways miss is about distance × angle (in radians):

1° at 5 m ≈ 8.7 cm sideways. 3° at 5 m is 26 cm, a third of a robot.

The same goes for a 1° pitch error in a camera mount (chapter 11).

Heading-constrained solves

Full PnP solves for 6 unknowns: x, y, z, roll, pitch and yaw. But on a flat field, the robot already knows most of them:

Full PnP: 6 unknowns

xyzrollpitchyaw

Everything comes from the corners, including the weak angle clue. One tag can give two answers.

Heading from the gyro: 2 unknowns

xyzrollpitchyaw

Height, roll and pitch are fixed: the robot sits on the floor and the camera mount is known. Yaw comes from the gyro. Only the position is left.

With just x and y to find, the four corners of one tag give 8 measurements for 2 unknowns: plenty. Even a single pair of corners is enough. The tag's direction in the image fixes the line the robot is on, and its size fixes how far along that line. And the flip can't happen: the flipped answer needs the robot to face a different way, and the gyro says it doesn't.

Limelight calls this MegaTag2. In PhotonLib it's estimateConstrainedSolvepnpPose (all tags, heading held to the gyro) or estimatePnpDistanceTrigSolvePose (the best single tag).

The robot's gyro knows its heading very accurately. Heading is exactly what vision is worst at from one tag, and far away a small angle error becomes a big miss: 1° at 5 m is about 8.7 cm sideways.

So let the gyro supply the heading. Then vision only has to find x and y: one tag, even two of its corners, gives a steady position, and the ambiguity flip can't happen, because it needs the robot to face a different way. The heading has to be the one from when the frame was captured, which PhotonLib looks up for you (chapter 14). Compare it with solving all six numbers from the corners (full PnP):

Why this works so well

The heading-constrained solve doesn't ask vision the question it's worst at. It uses vision only for what it's good at, direction and distance, and asks the gyro the question the gyro is best at. Each sensor covers the other's weakness.

Pitfalls in PhotonLib 2027 alpha-2
  • Missing heading throws. estimateConstrainedSolvepnpPose with headingFree=true calls headingBuffer.getSample(...).get() without checking, so it throws if no heading has been added yet. With headingFree=false it returns empty instead. Call addHeadingData before the first estimate either way (upstream fix: PhotonVision #2529).
  • CPU on the robot. The constrained solve runs on the SystemCore and is documented at "typically not more than 2 ms" on a roboRIO. Four cameras at about 2 frames each per loop is about 8 solves. Run it on the newest frame per camera, or use the trig solve, and time it on the SystemCore.
  • It needs the calibration. The constrained solve takes camera.getCameraMatrix() and camera.getDistCoeffs(). The distortion is 8 coefficients (N8), which matches our 8-coefficient fix. Both are empty until the camera has connected.
  • Trig solve uses one tag, the best target, even when several are visible.
  • Feed it fast. Our plan feeds addHeadingData every loop, ideally from the 250 Hz odometry-thread samples, so the buffer has a sample close to every capture time.
// every loop (or every odometry sample) estimator.addHeadingData(timestamp, drivetrain.getHeading()); // per result: MT2-style, heading held to the gyro var est = estimator.estimateConstrainedSolvepnpPose(result, camera.getCameraMatrix().get(), camera.getDistCoeffs().get(), seedPose, false /* headingFree */, headingScale); if (est.isEmpty()) est = estimator.estimatePnpDistanceTrigSolvePose(result);
How other teams use the gyro
  • 4533's Whacknet (a PhotonVision fork) does the heading-constrained solve on the coprocessor. The robot streams its gyro to it at 200 Hz over UDP, and the heading is interpolated to the shutter time. It's our upgrade path if the constrained solve turns out too heavy for the SystemCore.
  • AOS (Austin Schuh's system, run by 1868 and 4646) goes beyond MegaTag2: an extended Kalman filter on the coprocessor fuses 4 cameras, the robot's heading and its chassis speeds into one estimate of x, y and heading. Adopting it would mean replacing our robot-side localization.
  • 971's bos resolves single-tag ambiguity across cameras: it tries every combination of each camera's two candidate poses and keeps the set that agrees best with itself and with the last pose.
  • Limelight 4 has a built-in IMU, but on our robot it wasn't doing anything for the pose: in the mode we ran, it was just kept matched to our gyro, and MT2 still used the heading we pushed.
